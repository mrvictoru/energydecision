\begin{abstract}
Grid-scale batteries in Australia's National Electricity Market must co-optimise
energy arbitrage with eight frequency-control ancillary service (FCAS) markets
while paying for battery wear. We explore whether an offline sequence model ---
a Decision Transformer --- can act as a single, generalising controller for this
multi-market dispatch problem: learning from logged data rather than risky online
exploration, and optimising revenue net of degradation. We first train it on
trajectories generated by online reinforcement-learning policies (PPO and other
actor--critic agents). That route stalls at a \emph{behaviour-cloning ceiling}:
the logged policies bid little into FCAS, and no amount of larger or
better-curated data, return-to-go prompting, loss re-weighting, or online
fine-tuning lets the Decision Transformer exceed the behaviour present in its
logs. We therefore change the data source rather than the learner, distilling an
honest, non-clairvoyant stochastic-dynamic-programming planning teacher into a
standalone Decision Transformer and conditioning it on a state-dependent
return-to-go prompt derived from the planner's cost-to-go. The resulting policy
beats a tuned PPO baseline on four held-out surfaces (standard, dispatch-matched,
expanded broad-2024, and 2025 out-of-distribution) by $3.84\times$, $1.80\times$,
$6.70\times$, and $6.39\times$, with all four paired-difference 95\% confidence
intervals excluding zero, and passes a market-impact gate on all nine grid-scale
cells with no solver in the control loop. Results are simulator-based; sim-to-real
transfer is the main open question.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Grid-scale batteries are now a material part of Australia's National Electricity
Market (NEM), earning revenue both from energy arbitrage and from Frequency
Control Ancillary Services (FCAS), eight reserve markets that a single
controller must bid into simultaneously. Every five minutes the controller
chooses a nine-dimensional action: how much to charge or discharge, and how much
capacity to offer into each reserve service, while accounting for the wear each
cycle imposes on the battery. This is a sequential decision problem under
uncertainty, and it is in principle well suited to reinforcement learning
\citep{subramanya2022review}.

The practical obstacle is that online reinforcement learning learns by
exploration. In a live market, exploration is expensive, risky, and slow to
converge, and the value is concentrated in rare events (energy price spikes
and FCAS scarcity prices) that a learner must experience to price. Learning
from logged data avoids exploration entirely. The Decision Transformer (DT) is
the leading offline sequence-modelling approach: it is trained on fixed
trajectories with no environment interaction, and it conditions its actions on a
return-to-go (RTG) prompt describing how much value should remain
\citep{chen2021decision}. Offline learning, however, inherits the behaviour of
its data: a model trained by behaviour cloning cannot reliably produce actions
it has never seen.

We set out to test whether an offline sequence model can serve as a single,
generalising controller for this problem --- one that learns from logged data,
needs no online exploration, and optimises revenue net of degradation. We begin
with the most natural data source, trajectories generated by online
reinforcement-learning policies (PPO and other actor--critic agents), and that
route stalls at a \emph{behaviour-cloning ceiling}
(Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling}): across a systematic search within the cloning
paradigm (larger and better-curated offline datasets; RTG prompting; loss
re-weighting; mixed action heads; architectural changes; and online
fine-tuning), the DT never exceeds the FCAS-bidding behaviour present in its
logged data, and stays well below a tuned proximal-policy-optimisation (PPO)
baseline on broad and out-of-distribution surfaces. The ceiling is a property of
the data, not of the model.

Our contributions are threefold:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Diagnosis.} We isolate the behaviour-cloning ceiling and show
  that no within-cloning lever removes it (Figure~\ref{fig:ceiling};
  Appendix~\ref{app:ceiling}).
  \item \textbf{Method.} We break it by distilling an \emph{honest},
  non-clairvoyant stochastic-dynamic-programming (SDP) planning teacher
  \citep{abdulla2016optimal} into a standalone DT, following the imitation-learning
  recipe of \citet{ross2011reduction}, and conditioning the student on a
  \emph{state-dependent} RTG prompt derived from the planner's cost-to-go
  $J_t(\mathrm{soc})$ (Section~\ref{sec:method}). No solver runs in the control
  loop at deployment.
  \item \textbf{Evaluation.} We introduce a market-impact gate that re-prices the
  battery's own dispatch endogenously, and show that the value prompt must be
  gated by market power: the shipped policy passes the gate on all nine
  grid-scale cells (Section~\ref{sec:impact}).
\end{itemize}

On four held-out surfaces, the standalone DT beats tuned PPO by 3.84$\times$ on
standard 2024, 1.80$\times$ on dispatch-matched, 6.70$\times$ on expanded
broad-2024, and 6.39$\times$ on 2025 out-of-distribution data, with all four
paired-difference 95\% confidence intervals excluding zero
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}); relative to its solver-in-the-loop teacher on
the same surfaces it retains 77\% of the teacher's aggregate profit on standard
2024 and 70\% on dispatch-matched, and \emph{exceeds} it on expanded broad-2024
(132\%) and 2025 out-of-distribution (276\%), where the teacher's
seasonal-forecast planning generalises less well, all while removing the
solver from the control loop. All
results are simulator-based and driven by historical NEM data; sim-to-real
transfer is the main open question. Code, trajectory corpora, and model weights
are released with this paper.

\section{Related Work}

Sequence modelling reframes control as autoregressive prediction over
(return, state, action) tokens. The Decision Transformer conditions on a target
return-to-go and predicts actions, matching or exceeding online RL on several
offline benchmarks \citep{chen2021decision}; related work applies the recipe
online \citep{zheng2022online}, to value-based relabeling \citep{yamagata2023qdt}, and
to whole-trajectory modelling \citep{janner2021offline}. We adopt the Decision
Transformer and, unlike prior work, derive its RTG prompt from a planner's
cost-to-go and gate it by market power.

Offline value-based RL instead constrains or re-weights the value function to
avoid over-estimating unseen actions, as in conservative Q-learning
\citep{kumar2020cql} and implicit Q-learning \citep{kostrikov2022iql}. These are
the natural alternatives to sequence modelling; we chose the Decision Transformer
for its inference-time RTG conditioning, and leave a direct IQL/CQL comparison to
future work.

Distilling a planner into a reactive policy is classical in imitation learning,
where DAgger aggregates expert corrections under the student's own state
distribution \citep{ross2011reduction}. Our teacher is an honest, non-clairvoyant
stochastic-dynamic-programming planner over a seasonal price forecast
\citep{abdulla2016optimal}. Because it never sees realised future prices, the
distilled policy is more robust out-of-distribution than one trained on a
perfect-foresight planner.

Battery scheduling is a mature application area: dynamic-programming and
model-predictive approaches optimise operation against forecasts and
degradation \citep{abdulla2016optimal}, and detailed aging models quantify the
wear those schedules induce \citep{muenzel2015multifactor,kampker2025modelling};
reinforcement learning has been applied to storage in a range of settings
\citep{subramanya2022review}. Our setting differs in scope: a nine-dimensional
co-optimisation over energy and eight FCAS services, evaluated across multiple
held-out surfaces with an explicit market-impact gate.

\section{Experimental Framework}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Market environment}

We model a grid-scale battery participating in Australia's National Electricity
Market (NEM), using the \texttt{AEMOBatteryTradingEnv} environment. The
environment advances in five-minute intervals and exposes an 18-dimensional
observation: calendar features (hour-of-day and day-of-year sine/cosine
encodings and a peak-period indicator), the regional reference price (RRP),
total regional demand, the eight frequency-control ancillary service (FCAS)
prices, the solar and wind generation
fractions, and the battery state of charge. The action is nine-dimensional: one
energy-dispatch command in $[-1,1]$ (positive charges, negative discharges) and
eight FCAS bids in $[0,1]$, one per service (raise and lower regulation, and the
6-second, 60-second, and 5-minute contingency services). The reward is the
per-step profit in dollars: energy revenue plus FCAS revenue minus degradation
cost, with a small penalty for any state-of-charge-limit violation.

\subsection{Battery and degradation model}

Each battery is specified by an energy capacity, a power limit, and an initial
state of charge. Training uses four configurations spanning 2--50\,MWh, at a
constant cell temperature of $30^\circ$C. Battery wear is modelled with a
real-world calendar-and-cycle aging model: calendar aging from time- and
temperature-driven solid-electrolyte-interphase growth (Arrhenius temperature
dependence), and cycle aging that grows as a power law in depth of discharge and
increases with the C-rate (charge/discharge rate). We use the
lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) preset; the combined calendar-plus-cycle
formulation follows \citet{kampker2025modelling}, and the multi-factor
cycle-life component follows \citet{muenzel2015multifactor}. Wear is charged to the policy as part of the
environment reward and reported as a cost per unit of throughput.

\subsection{Market-impact model}

By default the battery is a price taker: it trades at the observed historical
RRP and FCAS prices, which its own dispatch does not move. For impact-aware
evaluation we use a piecewise-merit-order model in which the battery's dispatch
changes the realised prices (charging adds demand and raises the energy price,
discharging adds supply and lowers it), and each FCAS price is reduced in
proportion to the battery's share of an estimated service depth. This is a
per-interval approximation of an endogenous market, not a full market
re-clear. We call evaluations without this model \emph{identity} surfaces and
those with it \emph{impact} surfaces; Section~\ref{sec:impact} reports the impact
gate.

\subsection{Data}

The simulator is driven by historical AEMO market data: dispatch prices for
energy and the eight FCAS services, regional demand, and generation by fuel
type. Training trajectories and the seasonal price profile are drawn from
pre-2024 windows; evaluation uses 2024 (identity surfaces) and 2025
(out-of-distribution) windows that are held out by date.

\label{sec:method}

\subsection{SDP planner as teacher}

Dynamic programming solves a finite-horizon control problem by working
backward from a terminal cost. At each time and state, it selects a feasible
action that minimises the immediate cost plus the cost-to-go of the resulting
state. In battery dispatch, the state includes the battery's state of charge
(SOC), and the actions control charging or discharging. The general
battery-planning formulation is related to prior work on SDP-based storage
scheduling \citep{abdulla2016optimal}; here, we describe the specific
non-clairvoyant teacher used to generate our trajectories.

For energy dispatch, the teacher constructs a regional reference price (RRP)
forecast by averaging historical RRP within month-of-year and hour-of-day
bins. The profile is estimated from pre-2024 processed data and indexed by
the settlement times in each episode. The energy planner therefore uses
seasonal mean prices, not the episode's realised future RRP. FCAS is handled
separately: the bidding rule uses prices available at the current step and
does not forecast future FCAS prices. We use ``non-clairvoyant'' to describe
this restricted information set, in contrast to a planner given realised
future prices. For example, the NSW1 October seasonal profile gives mean RRP
values of about A\$77/MWh at 07:00 and A\$184/MWh at 18:00. The teacher
therefore expects lower-priced morning energy and higher-priced evening energy,
while actual prices on any particular day may differ substantially from those
historical means.

The teacher is a three-stage pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{SOC waypoints.} A learned model proposes a sequence of
  intermediate SOC targets. It supplies targets to the planner but does not
  optimise the dispatch actions.
  \item \textbf{Energy dispatch.} For each interval between waypoints, a
  discretised dynamic program performs backward induction over SOC states
  and candidate charge/discharge actions. It minimises forecast energy cost
  plus estimated degradation cost, with a soft terminal penalty that
  encourages the final SOC to approach the interval's target. The wear
  estimate combines the planner's degradation estimator with a linear
  throughput surrogate; the estimator is scaled by
  \texttt{deg\_calibration}.
  \item \textbf{FCAS bidding.} Given the energy action, a greedy rule
  allocates residual raise and lower headroom to the highest-priced eligible
  service in each direction, using current FCAS prices.
\end{enumerate}
Thus, the teacher combines learned waypoint selection, dynamic-programming
energy planning, and rule-based FCAS bidding. It is not a pure SDP solver
or a joint optimiser of energy and FCAS.

We also compute a free-terminal cost-to-go table $J_t(s)$ over time $t$ and
discretised SOC $s$:
\begin{equation}
  J_t(s) \;=\; \min_{a \in \mathcal{A}(s)}
  \left[ c_t(s,a) + J_{t+1}(s') \right],
  \qquad J_T(s) = 0,
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}(s)$ is the set of feasible actions at SOC $s$, $s'$ is
the resulting next SOC, and $c_t$ includes forecast energy cost and the
degradation surrogate. Unlike the waypoint-constrained dispatch plan, this
table has no terminal SOC target. At inference, the table is queried at the
current time and SOC, and the Decision Transformer receives $-J_t(s)$ as its
return-to-go prompt. The table provides a value-conditioning signal, not the
SDP policy: the student selects actions in a single forward pass, with no
solver in the control loop. The table itself is built by the same
seasonal dynamic program; because it uses no realised prices, it can be
precomputed and cached per region, season, and battery.

\subsection{Trajectory corpus}

We generated the student-training corpus by sampling random, fixed-length
windows from cached, preprocessed five-minute AEMO data and rolling out the
hybrid teacher described above. The corpus spans five NEM regions, two
episode horizons (3,456 steps, or 12 days; and 16,128 steps, or 8 weeks),
and four battery configurations, with eight episodes per region--horizon--battery
combination. This yields 320 episodes. The shipped conservative, wear-calibrated
corpus contains approximately 3.13 million per-step rows. Each episode is
generated by running the waypoint model, SDP energy planner, and greedy FCAS
bidder in the environment, so its observations, teacher actions, and rewards
come from the same rollout rather than being assembled from separate sources
\cite{generate_sdp_dt_trajectories}.

Each row stores the normalised 18-dimensional observation, the 9-dimensional
action (one energy-dispatch component and eight FCAS bids), and the resulting
per-step reward, together with episode and step identifiers and metadata.
For the shipped corpus, a per-step \texttt{rtg\_value} is also provided:
it is computed as $-J_t(s)$ from the free-terminal cost-to-go table, not as
a logged discounted sum of future rewards. The exported rows do not retain
timestamps. Accordingly, temporal non-overlap between training and evaluation
was checked against the source data windows, rather than by auditing
timestamps stored in each corpus row.
\subsection{Student Decision Transformer}

The student is a causal Decision Transformer trained on the teacher-generated
sequences described above. At each step, it embeds the return-to-go, normalised
observation, and action as an ordered sequence and uses the preceding context
to predict actions. The shipped model has 8 Transformer blocks with hidden
dimension 768, grouped-query attention with 12 query heads and 6 key--value
heads, QK-Norm, rotary positional embeddings, and SwiGLU feed-forward layers.
Its context covers 210 time steps. A mixed action head applies a hyperbolic
tangent to the energy-dispatch output, which lies in $[-1,1]$, and a sigmoid to
the eight FCAS-bid outputs, which lie in $[0,1]$. Training minimises a
weighted combination of action, state, and return prediction losses:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}
  = w_a\,\mathrm{MSE}_a
  + w_s\,\mathrm{MSE}_s
  + w_r\,\mathrm{MSE}_r,
  \qquad
  (w_a,w_s,w_r)=(0.999,\,0.002,\,0.0001).
\end{equation}
The loss is therefore dominated by action prediction, while state and return
prediction provide lower-weight auxiliary objectives. To keep the RTG inputs
numerically scaled for training, we use an automatically calibrated
\texttt{return\_scale}; for the shipped checkpoint it is 23{,}284.83, which maps
return-to-go values to order-10 magnitudes for the network.
\subsection{Return-to-go prompting and \texttt{auto} mode}

At inference time, the Decision Transformer is conditioned on a return-to-go
(RTG) prompt derived from the teacher's free-terminal value table. Specifically,
we set $\mathrm{RTG} = -J_t(s)$, normalised by the checkpoint's
\texttt{return\_scale}, so that higher remaining value corresponds to a larger
prompt token. This turns the SDP cost-to-go into a value-conditioning signal
while keeping the control loop solver-free: the student selects actions
with a single forward pass. The value table is produced by a single
backward-induction pass per episode, not per decision, and because it is a
function only of the seasonal forecast and the SOC grid (no realised prices), it
can be precomputed offline and cached per region, season, and battery, leaving
only a constant-time lookup at each step. Figure~\ref{fig:jtsoc} shows the table
for a representative window.

For price-taking evaluation surfaces, the shipped inference mode \texttt{auto}
resolves to the state-dependent \texttt{j\_t\_soc} prompt: the agent looks up
$J_t(s)$ for the current time and SOC and feeds its negative, scaled by
\texttt{return\_scale}, to the transformer as RTG. Under a market-impact model
(Section~\ref{sec:impact}), where the battery's own dispatch moves prices,
\texttt{auto} instead falls back to a constant \texttt{rtg0.0} prompt. This is a
robustness choice, not a failure mode: on large batteries, a value prompt
that is too optimistic can over-trade energy and self-suppress realised prices,
so the conservative constant prompt is used once the battery is no longer a
negligible price taker. Using the state-dependent prompt directly in this regime,
rather than falling back to a constant, remains an open item.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
We evaluate the student on four held-out surfaces. The \emph{standard 2024}
surface contains six periods from each of five regions ($n=30$ scenario
cells). The \emph{dispatch-matched} surface covers all twelve months of
SA1 2024 ($n=12$). The \emph{expanded broad-2024} surface spans five
regions and six periods ($n=30$). Finally, the \emph{2025 OOD} surface
evaluates six NSW1, QLD1, and SA1 scenario cells from January and February
($n=6$). Training data and the seasonal price profile use pre-2024 source
windows; the evaluation windows are held out by date.

We compare the Decision Transformer with a tuned PPO policy, an
\texttt{fcas\_rule} policy, and dispatch replay (the latter only on the
dispatch-matched surface). PPO is tuned using an
Optuna search over ten trials and trained for up to four million
environment steps. Evaluation uses five-minute steps and one rollout per
scenario--battery cell. PPO and rule-policy reference rollouts are run
against the same evaluation scenarios as the student, with fresh reference
caches to keep the comparisons consistent.

We report mean episode profit and paired differences between the student
and PPO. Uncertainty in the paired differences is estimated by bootstrap
resampling the matched scenario cells with 10{,}000 replicates; paired
Wilcoxon tests are reported as a complementary analysis. We treat the
paired-difference confidence interval as the primary inferential result.
Scenario cells from the same region may be correlated, so the cell count
should not be interpreted as an equal number of independent regions.
The student is deterministic at evaluation time; baseline reference
rollouts can vary slightly run-to-run (see Table~\ref{tab:headline}).

\section{Market-Impact Gate}
\label{sec:impact}

To test robustness to endogenous prices we use the piecewise-merit-order model
of Section~\ref{sec:setup}: energy prices shift with the battery's net dispatch
(charging raises demand, discharging raises supply), and FCAS prices are
attenuated by the battery's share of estimated service depth. This is a
per-interval approximation of price impact, not a full market re-clear.

The gate comprises nine matched scenario--battery cells: three grid-scale
batteries (small, Hornsdale-class, and Torrens Island-class) in three scenarios
(SA1 October and November, VIC1 October). Under market impact, \texttt{auto}
uses the fixed \texttt{rtg0.0} prompt rather than the state-dependent
$J_t(s)$ prompt, and we compare this shipped fallback with PPO on the same
cells. The Decision Transformer earns more than PPO in all nine cells. Across
the nine paired differences, its mean advantage is \$167{,}099 per episode
(95\% bootstrap confidence interval [\$101{,}213, \$227{,}362]). At $n=9$ the
two-sided Wilcoxon minimum is $0.0039$, which the paired test attains; the
bootstrap interval is the primary evidence. The ratio of mean profits is
4.36$\times$ for the small battery, 4.71$\times$ for Hornsdale, and
4.67$\times$ for Torrens Island. For the price-taking counterpart, see the
identity-surface comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

These results support a mode-selection rule, not a claim that one prompt is
best in every regime: use the state-dependent value prompt when the battery
is approximately price-taking, and the conservative constant prompt when
its dispatch can materially affect prices. In this sense, market power is
a gate on value-based prompting. Figure~\ref{fig:impact} summarises the gate.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Simulator-based.} All results come from our simulator, driven by
historical NEM data; sim-to-real transfer is the main open item. The
market-impact model is a per-interval merit-order approximation, not a full
market re-clear.

\textbf{Same-simulator teacher and evaluation.} The teacher and the evaluation
share the simulator, so the student may inherit environment-specific behaviour.
Held-out 2025 data and the endogenous-price impact gate mitigate this, but the
circularity is reduced, not removed.

\textbf{Baseline strength and noise.} The PPO reference is tuned (Optuna, up to
four million steps), but its rollouts vary slightly run-to-run at long horizons
($\sim$1\%), whereas the student is deterministic. We therefore treat the
paired-difference confidence interval as the primary evidence and report cell
counts honestly.

\textbf{Sample size.} Some surfaces have few cells (as few as six), and cells
from the same region are correlated, so the cell count overstates the number of
independent regions. The paired-difference bootstrap is the primary inferential
tool; the Wilcoxon test is indicative at these sizes.

\textbf{Sensitivity to physics.} Correcting the shared degradation and
efficiency models moved the headline numbers substantially. The shipped results
use the corrected physics; several earlier numbers are superseded and are not
relied upon here.

\textbf{Retention and scope.} Distillation is not uniformly lossy. The student
retains 70--77\% of its solver-in-the-loop teacher's aggregate profit on the
in-distribution surfaces (standard, dispatch-matched) and \emph{exceeds} it on
the broad and out-of-distribution surfaces, where the teacher's seasonal-forecast
planning generalises less well. Any single retention figure is therefore
surface-dependent, and the per-cell ratios are noisier than the aggregates. The
approach also does not transfer automatically to the residential scale, where the
same recipe over-cycles on long horizons.

\textbf{Provenance.} The shipped checkpoint's \texttt{return\_scale}
(23{,}284.83) is the correct value for inference, but it does not reproduce
exactly as $\mathrm{p90}(|J_t|)/10$ of the released corpus, suggesting the
released corpus may differ slightly from the file used at training; the
discrepancy is documented in the released audit notes.

\section{Conclusion}

We set out to test whether an offline sequence model could serve as a single,
generalising controller for multi-market battery dispatch, learning from logged
data without online exploration and optimising revenue net of degradation.
Training on trajectories from online reinforcement-learning policies stalled at a
behaviour-cloning ceiling: a systematic search within the cloning paradigm never
exceeded the FCAS-bidding behaviour present in the logged data (C1), so the
ceiling is a property of the data, not of the model. We
broke it by distilling an honest, non-clairvoyant SDP planning teacher into a
standalone Decision Transformer prompted with a state-dependent cost-to-go
$J_t(\mathrm{soc})$ (C2), so the student roughly matches the teacher's aggregate
profit on the in-distribution surfaces and exceeds it out-of-distribution, with
no solver in the control loop. We then showed that value-based prompting must be
gated by market power: the shipped surface-aware policy passes a piecewise
merit-order impact gate on all nine grid-scale cells (C3).

Overall, the results argue that offline sequence modelling is a credible
control prior for utility-scale battery trading when the data source is chosen
well --- most of the planner, none of the solver in the control loop. The main
open question is sim-to-real transfer, which we leave to a shadow-mode
deployment and to extending the market-impact model beyond its per-interval
approximation.
